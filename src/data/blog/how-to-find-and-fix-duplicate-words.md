When drafting an essay, article, or business proposal, your brain naturally falls into comfortable linguistic patterns. You reach for a familiar adjective like *effective* or *important*, and without realizing it, you use that same word four times across two short paragraphs.

This is completely normal during first drafts. However, leaving repetitive vocabulary unedited leads to reader fatigue and weakens the impact of your ideas.

Here is a practical guide on how to identify repetitive vocabulary, catch accidental duplicate words, and sharpen your copy.

## The Two Types of Duplicate Words

Before editing, it helps to distinguish between the two distinct kinds of duplicate words:

### 1. Accidental Back-to-Back Words (Typographical Slips)
These are typos where the exact same word is typed twice in succession (for example: *"We went to **the the** store"* or *"It is **is** ready"*).

These usually happen when:
- Your thoughts move faster than your fingers on the keyboard.
- You cut and paste a sentence fragment into an existing line.
- You edit on a mobile phone with aggressive predictive text.

Because the human brain reads words in contextual chunks rather than letter-by-letter, your eyes will frequently glide right past double words during self-editing.

### 2. Crutch Words (Repetitive Vocabulary)
Crutch words are vocabulary terms writers lean on subconsciously throughout a document. Rather than appearing side-by-side, they show up repeatedly across paragraphs.

Common offenders include:
- **Generic Intensifiers:** *very, really, extremely, literally, definitely*
- **Filler Adverbs:** *actually, basically, essentially, honestly*
- **Vague Nouns:** *things, aspects, solutions, factors, elements*
- **Overused Corporate Verbs:** *utilize, leverage, facilitate, streamline*

While grammatically correct, repeating these terms dulls your writing and makes thoughtful ideas sound generic.

## How to Find Duplicate Words Step-by-Step

Instead of reading your draft ten times and hoping your eyes catch every repetition, you can run a quick lexical frequency check:

1. **Copy your draft** from your word processor, Google Docs, or CMS.
2. **Open Casetivo's [Duplicate Word Finder](/duplicate-word-finder).**
3. **Toggle "Ignore Common Stop Words."** This filters out natural grammatical connecting words (*the, and, in, of, to*) so your list focuses strictly on nouns, adjectives, and verbs.
4. **Click "Find Duplicate Words."**
5. **Review the frequency ranking.** Look at any non-technical word that appears more than three or four times.

## Practical Editing Examples

Here is how replacing or cutting overused words immediately strengthens a sentence:

### Example 1: Cutting Empty Intensifiers
- **Draft:** *"The onboarding process is **very** simple, and our customer support is **very** helpful."*
- **Edited:** *"The onboarding process is **straightforward**, and our support team is **responsive**."*

### Example 2: Varying Overused Verbs
- **Draft:** *"We **utilize** modern analytics to **utilize** our data effectively."*
- **Edited:** *"We **harness** modern analytics to **interpret** our data effectively."*

### Example 3: Fixing Accidental Double Words
- **Draft:** *"Make sure that **that** all team members are notified."*
- **Edited:** *"Ensure all team members are notified."*

## When Word Repetition Is Actually Good

Not all word repetition should be eliminated. In fact, editing out words too aggressively can sometimes make writing harder to read.

Keep words identical when:
- **You are using technical terms:** If your software uses an API key, calling it an *"API token"*, *"secret credential"*, or *"access password"* in different sections will only confuse the reader. Stick with the exact term.
- **You are using intentional rhetorical repetition:** Repeating a phrase deliberately at the start of consecutive sentences (anaphora) creates rhythm and emphasis in speeches and persuasive copy.
- **The alternative synonym is obscure:** Never swap a simple, common word for a clunky, archaic synonym just to avoid repeating it. Clarity always beats a thesaurus flex.

## Quick Self-Editing Checklist

Before you hit publish or submit your paper, run through this quick 60-second review:

- [ ] Run the text through the [Duplicate Word Finder](/duplicate-word-finder) with stop words ignored.
- [ ] Check the top 3 most repeated words—can you swap at least two instances for punchier synonyms?
- [ ] Scan for back-to-back duplicate words (*in in*, *to to*, *the the*).
- [ ] If formatting headlines, ensure they follow proper [Title Case](/title-case) rather than haphazard capitalization.
- [ ] Read the text out loud once. Your ears will often catch awkward repetition that your eyes miss.
